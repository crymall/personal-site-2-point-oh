I ran 10 kilometers today for the first time in a decade. 

I started running every day back in my mid-20s. It helped me cope during a hard time for my mental health. I kept it up for a year or two, long enough to get me through. Then came a full-time job, then the pandemic, and eventually, my running habit got lost in the shuffle of daily life.

Last year, 34 years old and frustrated by my declining physical ability, I decided to start going to the gym. Every gym day, I'd attempt to run a mile, then I'd lift weights til my limbs felt like noodles. As I shaped up, those warm-up runs started to feel better, so I started replacing 1 day a week with pure running, longer distances, just me and the treadmill.

First, I ran 5 kilometers. My legs and feet hurt, adjusting to the longer distance, but thankfully, my months of running single miles had paid off, and the cardio was doable. Then I ramped up to 5 miles. Similar adjustment. Today, I proved to myself that I can do 10 kilometers. My time for 10 kilometers was 61 minutes exactly, which is under 10 minutes a mile.

These are just numbers. I've been trying to cultivate the ability to notice these numbers without evaluating them. If you are moved to congratulate, congratulations are not in order. I'm not doing it for anyone. Eventually, I wasn't even doing it for myself. I was doing it to maintain the practice of doing it. One day, early on, I was complaining to my therapist about not being motivated to do x, y, or z, and she told me: "If you only do it when you're motivated, you won't do it." This is true, I've found. Habits form when the activity becomes a perpetual motion machine, self-sufficient, without input even from the practitioner.

Getting to that point is difficult. Runners will be the first to tell you that running sucks. When I'm out of shape, it feels bad, and even when I'm in shape, it doesn't always feel good.

Running isn't the only difficult habit I've been cultivating. I recently found a small group that gets together weekly to meditate. I'd been trying to meditate by myself, but was unable to be still for longer than 15 or so minutes at a time. I figured having some social pressure would help me stick with it.

The group meditation sessions are 45 minutes of sitting still and shutting up. It's an understanding group, so I'm allowed to reposition my body to remain comfortable, which I have to do, multiple times per session. Sometimes I have to clear my throat, and in my mind, it reverberates like a gunshot in a cathedral. No matter what, I sit stewing in worry that I'm doing it wrong, that there's somehow more to meditation than sitting in silence, that I should be undergoing some kind of profound internal reorientation toward perfect serenity.

Like running, meditation is physically and mentally taxing. Like running, it gets easier, but is always difficult. These are the vagaries of honest pursuits. I ran 10 kilometers today. Tomorrow, I will sit in silence for 45 minutes. Whether I do these things poorly or not, whether I cough or get charley horses or feel like I fucked up, if I managed to do them, I managed to do them.

Still, I don't like to think of these things as accomplishments. To have completed them, in a vacuum, is meaningless. Like Vonnegut's outing to buy envelopes, the point is in the doing. There's an overlooked, intangible, but profound meaning, long-term, in doing things. Running, meditating, dancing, cooking. Almost anything—but especially things that are difficult and incremental and never finished. I can't emphasize this part enough: It's not about getting things done. It's about doing things.

This is what LLMs are threatening to take away from us.

## From Cyborgs to Robots

I've always been equal parts in awe and annoyed by how quickly my mind trades natural capacity for artificial augmentation. Things that used to come naturally have been automated and atrophied, one after another, by the smartphone. Since they figured out how to find information on the Internet, I can feel my memory becoming a sieve that filters out everything on a subject but the most basic facts. With online maps and directions, I can spend years in a city and still be unable to do any independent wayfinding.

In this way, we're dulled. The things that make us who we are become excised and replaced by off-the-shelf cybernetic parts. Nobody is good or bad with directions anymore. Directions are handled by the smartphone. People still know stuff about things (for now), but the better-documented and more-searchable a subject is, the cheaper its information becomes, until eventually no one has any extrinsic incentive to know about it anymore.

This has been true since long before the advent of large language models, with one big difference: LLMs promise to trade the reliability that comes with manual input for the efficiency of letting the computer do everything for you. 

For example: Collaborations between online maps and human drivers, by and large, work as intended. The services are accurate, the inputs are not up to interpretation, and people generally get to their destination. As a thought exercise, let's imagine the AI-disrupted version of this: A service that promises to get you there effortlessly and exponentially quicker (think autonomous driving at high speeds), at the cost of it occasionally misinterpreting your instructions, making an error and ending up at the wrong place entirely.

It's not unreasonable, at first blush, to see this kind of trade-off as worthwhile. Many people see their travel time and labor as onerous. If they could teleport somewhere, they would. That's the problem this product is trying to solve. Sure, you might get sent to Basil instead of Berlin at first, but just tweak the prompt, run it again, and you'll be at the Bundestag in no time—still less time than it would have taken you to get there the old way, and without the effort. The loss of accuracy is unfortunate, an Altman or a Musk might say, but the efficiency gains are an an undeniable good.

But are they? The old way, my hands are on the wheel and my foot is on the pedal. The old way, I'm sure I'm getting to my destination, because I'm making incremental progress that I can understand. The old way, I can listen to music or chat with my passenger. The old way, if an animal pounces in front of the car, I don't have to rely on a machine's understanding of the trolley problem to avoid it.

The old way, I might be a cyborg, painstakingly following the instruction manual to use my new implant's capabilities, but at least I'm not a robot, getting a slightly-improved no-effort version of the same capability by trading my agency away. The old way, I'm doing something with enhancement. The new way, I'm not doing anything at all.

## Looking Outside the Frame

All of this critique is compelling inasmuch as we take Big AI's framing of their offerings at face value. AI isn't marketed as a tool so much as it's marketed as a *replacement* for tools. You aren't using a saw, you're telling an agent what to cut. The tool, if there can be said to be one, is the medium through which you explain and the model understands. These companies want us to believe that explaining to agents what to do is more efficient than doing it yourself. You can tell X agent what to cut while Y agent sands the other pieces and Z agent stains them, and so on, with a multiplicative productivity human effort can't replicate.

However, nearly anyone can get a ramshackle birdhouse by telling a mediocre woodworker what to do. To get them to build something nice, it takes an expert's direction and supervision. Because LLMs are regularly fallible, telling them where (and how) to cut a board can be just as painstaking as using the saw yourself. While they're sold to us as a replacement for costly expertise, in reality, a user needs a high level of expertise, and more than a little elbow grease, to coax quality output out of an LLM.

"Quality" is the operative word here. If we still care about quality, then we still care about craft, expertise, and process. LLMs by themselves can be useful for mocking things up, for creating a ghost of a final product, but they can't make something that stands up to scrutiny without close supervision by an expert hand. Which, if you still think that the experience of doing something is intrinsically valuable, thank goodness! This is an argument for process that should ring true even to those who only care about the bottom line.

In summary: I want to change how we view LLMs, and to use that new perspective to change how we use them. Rather than using them as a replacement for your own effort, use them as an upgrade to your old tools as you put in the effort yourself. Prompt the mindless stuff and fill in the rest manually. Teach the model what you want, and in teaching it, reinforce your own understanding of it. Rather than saying *build me an app that does x, y, and z*, say *what kind of architecture and toolset would you recommend for an app that needs to do x, y, and z?*, and then when it gives you an answer, think for yourself and use that as input to develop your own opinion on what the approach should be.

In this way, we can try to avoid the worst things that AI can do to our brains: Convince us that its effort, its production, is ours. That the process of making something is worthless inefficiency, that it's beneath us. That the sausage is an end in itself and we never have to worry about how it's made again.

## Caveat Promptor

This is a proposal, not a manifesto, so take everything with a grain of salt. To clarify:

I've only used LLMs in a software engineering context. I care about code quality and I'm proud of the code I write, regardless of the tools I use to produce it. I also understand that a compiler doesn't evaluate how elegant, unique, or special your code is, and that many codebases will end up looking awfully similar. This is why Rails had the `generate` command long before LLMs were a thing. Boilerplate is boilerplate, and a working app is a working app. This isn't to say that a human hand isn't a critical part of the process of writing functional, maintainable code. It's just to say that I don't romanticize the human hand in that context.

In other contexts, I'm much more skeptical of the utility of AI, and I'm much more protective of the role of the human, especially in communication, art, friendship, mental healthcare, and other pursuits where the direct audience of the output of your work is a human being and not a compiler. 

This is for two reasons. First, these pursuits tend to involve skills that are important for people to cultivate—for example: paying attention, using your senses, having and expressing empathy. An LLM can't do these things for you, and you shouldn't want them to. You should want to be human, and to be able to genuinely express your humanness to others. Second, because LLMs aren't good at doing these things, people can tell when they try, and it feels bad. It feels bad to get a generated email. It feels bad to realize that a piece of art is generated. It feels bad because the thing that generated it is incapable of the baseline sensory experiences and feelings that are foundational to all human expression.

While I've mostly focused on what AI does to our brains, and how it relates to living a good life, there are other concerns that must be acknowledged. Most models are trained on a huge corpus of copy-protected material, which is at best cavalier, at worst illegal, and in either case, unethical and arguably plagiarism. Industrial AI implementation is currently transforming the landscape of my home country, the United States. Massive private data centers are being built across the North American continent. These are facing stiff public opposition driven by concerns about water and power usage, tax breaks and other subsidies, and the promised versus actual economic benefits they'll provide.

These are real concerns, concerns that I share. Some are mitigated by running <a href="https://github.com/msb-msb/awesome-local-ai" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">local models</a>, but only somewhat. I started using LLMs to skill up to get a job as a software developer. Given that they're required for my work, I started thinking about how to use them as responsibly as possible. I'm also using LinkedIn a lot, and I've been seeing a lot of obviously-generated posts pontificating on AI in my feed—mostly people gushing about how useful it is, and how productive they've become because of it.

This is my version of an AI LinkedIn post. Except it's all handwritten. Don't let the em-dashes fool you. That's just how I write.

Thanks for reading.
